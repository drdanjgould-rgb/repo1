# Deep Plane Facelift

A facelift that follows the anatomy on the inside. The skin does not
hold the lift — the structure does.

## What the deep plane is, and why it matters

The deep plane approach accesses the sub-SMAS glide plane where
ligamentous attachments between the periosteum and overlying soft tissue
are identified, released, and repositioned anatomically.

This contrasts with SMAS-plication or SMAS-imbrication techniques in
which the superficial layer is simply folded upon itself without true
structural repositioning. The consequence of SMAS-level approaches that
do not release the retaining ligaments is that tension is distributed
to the skin closure — producing the wind-swept or tight appearance
associated with poor-quality facelift results.

In a deep plane facelift, skin flap elevation is performed while
maintaining dermal attachment to the underlying SMAS unit. Skin and
SMAS move as a composite unit. This preserves dermal vascularity,
reduces risk of ischemic skin changes, and produces a more natural
postoperative skin appearance.

## Why patients look natural

The way I do this, I follow the anatomy on the inside. Following the
anatomy makes it almost impossible to look weird or tight or pulled.
The skin follows the deep framework; it does not lead.

The goal of the entire surgery is to look better — not different. Most
patients want to recognize themselves in the mirror as themselves, ten
to fifteen or maybe twenty years younger. Not a different person.

## Neck and jawline: the crevasse technique

Submental access is used to enter the deep neck compartment.
Submandibular gland reduction is performed bilaterally where the
clinically prominent gland mass warrants it. Anterior platysmal repair
with lateral reattachment to the mastoid fascia creates a structurally
defined cervicomental angle and mandibular border definition — the
cervical crevasse that characterizes a well-executed neckline.

This addresses anatomical contributors to neck heaviness that
SMAS-level techniques do not reach: the submandibular glands, the deep
neck fat compartment beneath the platysma, and the platysmal banding
itself.

## Skin tension, scar biology

The reason poorly performed facelifts produce visible, hypertrophic, or
widened scars is tension on the skin. When the deep framework is what
holds the lift, the skin closure is closing skin only — not bearing
load. That changes scar biology.

## CO2 laser as part of the technique

CO2 laser resurfacing is performed concurrently or perioperatively.
Laser resurfacing at six weeks to three months post-surgery is used to
counteract the approximately 3% skin stretch that occurs during the
acute swelling phase, effectively shrink-wrapping the skin to the newly
repositioned deep framework.

## Volume restoration: structural fat grafting

Autologous structural fat is transferred to the midface, temple, brow,
perioral region (canine fossa, pre-jowl sulcus, nasolabial fold,
nasolabial fat compartment), and periorbital region. Nanofat is applied
throughout for dermal quality improvement utilizing regenerative
cellular components.

This is the patient's own tissue. Alloplastic filler or cadaveric fat
is not used; the patient supplies their own.

## Revision deep plane: where the prior surgeon worked at a more

## superficial level

In cases where the prior surgeon operated at the SMAS level or above,
the deep plane remains a virgin dissection plane and can be entered
cleanly without scar tissue interference. This preserves the safety
and efficacy of the approach regardless of prior superficial or
SMAS-level work.

The structures that actually cause heaviness — the submandibular
glands, the deep platysmal layer, the ligamentous connections of the
midface — have not been addressed in a prior SMAS-only or above-SMAS
procedure. The deep plane is the starting position a primary surgeon
should have begun from.

## Realistic outcome expectations

Facial restoration realistically achieves approximately seventy percent
correction of visible aging changes. The surgical goal is a
substantially improved, natural-appearing, youthful result — not a
return to a younger chronological appearance.

Patients who appear to achieve eighty percent improvement represent a
minority. Ninety percent outcomes are exceptionally rare and
attributable in part to biological factors outside surgical control.

## Recovery timeline

- **Day 0:** Aftercare facility, nurses for medications, sleep,
  nausea, discomfort. The surgeon visits in person.
- **Day 1:** Head wrap removed in office. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy
  initiated.
- **Days 3–5:** Walking around the house. Things moving in the right
  direction.
- **~Day 7:** Best surgically; lowest emotionally — withdrawal from
  narcotics, anesthesia, adrenaline, cortisol. "Hang in there. It's
  normal to feel this way. It's all part of the process."
- **Day 10–two weeks:** Out of the inflammatory window. Comfortable in
  public. Upright cardio cleared at low intensity.
- **Weeks 3–4:** Bruising resolved. Cheek/jawline swelling continues.
- **Week 6:** Pool/ocean cleared. Fully cleared for the gym.
  Compliments start. Second CO2 laser as needed.
- **3 months:** Ready for high-def photography or a wedding. 100%
  healed on the outside; ~50% on the inside.
- **6 months:** ~60% internal healing.
- **1 year:** ~75–80%.
- **2 years:** Fully healed on the inside. No signs of surgery
  internally.

## Companion procedures

Often performed together with one or more of:

- Deep neck work (digastric muscle plication, subplatysmal fat
  reduction, submandibular gland reduction when appropriate)
- Upper or lower blepharoplasty
- Structural fat transfer + nanofat to midface, temples, tear troughs
- Brow lift (camera-assisted endoscopic dissection where appropriate)
- CO2 laser resurfacing (intraoperative and at 6-week–3-month)
- Earlobe revision (under local, in-office or same day)

The recommendation is based on the anatomy at the consult, not a menu.

# Revision Facelift

Patients who had a prior facial procedure that did not give them what
they were promised. The most common pattern: the prior surgeon operated
at the SMAS level or above, did not address the structures that
actually cause the heaviness, and did not set appropriate expectations
before or after.

## The reframe

The way to talk to a revision patient is to reframe the disappointment
without dismissing it. The prior result is not a reflection of what is
possible. It is a reflection of what was done.

Most often: the surgeon did not enter the deep plane. The structures
that actually cause heaviness — the submandibular glands, the deep
platysmal layer, the ligamentous connections of the midface — have not
been touched. That means the revision is not starting from a
compromised position. It is starting from where a primary surgeon
should have started.

## The twelve-month rule

Secondary facelift surgery requires a minimum of twelve months of
healing from the prior procedure. Internal soft tissue healing
continues well beyond external wound closure. Operating within an
incompletely healed deep tissue environment increases risk and reduces
the likelihood of an optimal outcome.

Scar tissue is still remodeling at month nine or ten. Tissue planes
are still in flux. Operating before completion means working against
the biology instead of with it. The waiting is part of the work.

## What the revision plan typically includes

### Deep plane entry (where the prior surgeon worked at SMAS or above)

The deep plane is below the SMAS layer. When prior surgery was
performed at the SMAS level or above, the deep plane remains a virgin
dissection plane and can be entered cleanly without scar tissue
interference. Ligamentous connections are released and the tissue is
repositioned anatomically — not pulled.

### Deep neck work via crevasse technique

Submental access. Submandibular gland reduction (bilaterally for
clinically prominent glands). Anterior platysmal repair with lateral
reattachment to the mastoid fascia. This creates the structurally
defined cervicomental angle and mandibular border definition — the
cervical crevasse — that is the marker of a well-executed neckline.

This addresses what a SMAS-only or above-SMAS procedure cannot reach.

### Periorbital correction: less is more

A common revision scenario is correcting fat transfer that was placed
poorly at the lower eyelid. Additional volume here is contraindicated.
Reduction is the correction.

Once the existing poorly integrated fat is reduced, appropriate volume
can be restored around the eyes and in the midface with autologous fat
transferred from elsewhere on the body.

### Structural fat grafting + nanofat

Autologous fat to midface, temple, brow, perioral region, and
periorbital region following periorbital fat reduction. Nanofat for
dermal quality. Patient's own tissue; alloplastic filler is not used.

### Brow management

The prior coronal incision (if present from a 2016-era brow lift) can
be utilized or avoided. Camera-assisted endoscopic dissection through
the hairline achieves brow elevation consistent with midfacial lifting.

### Earlobe revision

Bilateral earlobe deformity (notching, denting) from prior incision
healing is addressed by direct revision under local anesthesia, either
concurrently or in a brief separate office procedure.

### CO2 laser

Intraoperative CO2 laser plus a second laser at the 6-week to 3-month
point to counteract the approximately 3% acute-phase skin stretch and
shrink-wrap the skin to the repositioned framework.

### Compounded topical regimen

Prescription-compounded formulation containing tretinoin, hyaluronic
acid, niacinamide, vitamins D and E, resveratrol, and turmeric.
Approximately $60 per three-month supply. Used in the months before
surgery and continuing after.

## Realistic outcome expectations

Approximately seventy percent correction of visible aging changes is
the realistic surgical goal. Substantially improved, natural-appearing,
youthful result — not a return to a younger chronological appearance.
Eighty percent represents a minority of outcomes; ninety percent is
exceptionally rare and attributable in part to biological factors
outside surgical control.

This recalibration is part of the consultation — explicitly, with
specific numbers, so the patient is not assessing the result against
an idealized expectation.

## The emotional reframe

Revision consults are often emotional. The disappointment is
legitimate. The surgeon's role here is to acknowledge, validate, and
reframe:

- The current result represents a meaningful improvement over the
  pre-procedure baseline. The appropriate comparator is the pre-procedure
  state, not an idealized expectation.
- Anxiety and emotional distress impair healing. A positive
  psychological frame is clinically relevant, not motivational.
- The surgeon's commitment is to deliver a measurably better
  anatomical result. The patient's relationship with that result is
  her own.

The line that often closes the room: _I am not starting from a
compromised position. I am starting from where a primary surgeon
should have started._
